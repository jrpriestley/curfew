# The TV screensaver's facts about the internet and computing: one a line, shown in turn
# (curfew.web.pages.flow). Keep them true, and short enough for two lines.
The first message over ARPANET, UCLA to Stanford in October 1969, was “LO”: the system crashed before “LOGIN” was finished.
The first spam went out in 1978: an advert for DEC computers, to about 400 ARPANET users.
Scott Fahlman proposed :-) for jokes on a Carnegie Mellon message board in 1982.
The first website went live at CERN in 1991, and it's still there: info.cern.ch.
ping is named after sonar. Mike Muuss wrote it in an evening in 1983.
traceroute sends packets set to die after 1, 2, 3… hops, and maps the path from each router's “time exceeded” complaint.
There are 13 root server names, a to m, but well over a thousand machines around the world answer for them.
On 1 January 1983, “flag day”, ARPANET switched to TCP/IP overnight: the start of the internet as we know it.
Before DNS (Paul Mockapetris, 1983), every computer's address came from one shared file, HOSTS.TXT.
HTTP 418 “I'm a teapot” comes from an April Fools' RFC in 1998.
RFC 1149 sends IP packets by carrier pigeon. A Norwegian group actually tried it in 2001.
Ethernet was invented at Xerox PARC in 1973; it first ran at 2.94 Mb/s.
The first Wi-Fi standard, 802.11 in 1997, topped out at 2 Mb/s.
An Ethernet frame carries at most 1,500 bytes: the internet moves in pieces about a paragraph long.
10.x.x.x addresses like this house's are set aside by RFC 1918: the internet never routes them.
A MAC address's first half names its maker. curfew uses it to guess what each device is.
Port 443 carries the encrypted web, 80 the plain one, 53 DNS, 22 SSH.
Bluetooth is named after Harald Bluetooth, a 10th-century Danish king; its logo joins his initials in runes.
The computer “bug”: in 1947 a moth was found in Harvard's Mark II and taped into the log book.
The NSFNET backbone ran at 56 kb/s in 1986: slower than a phone's text messages feel today.
The first YouTube video, “Me at the zoo” (2005), is 19 seconds long.
CAPTCHA stands for Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart.
The .tv domain belongs to Tuvalu, a Pacific island nation that earns real money from it.
Google is named after a googol: 10 to the power of 100.
Light in fibre covers about 200 km each millisecond: no reply from across the Atlantic comes back in much under 60 ms.
About 99% of the traffic between continents travels through cables on the sea floor, not satellites.
One cable, 2Africa, runs about 45,000 km around a continent.
The first webcam, at Cambridge in 1991, watched a coffee pot, so nobody walked to an empty one.
Ray Tomlinson picked the @ sign for email in 1971, twenty years before the web.
The first .com name, symbolics.com, was registered on 15 March 1985.
IPv4 has about 4.3 billion addresses, fewer than there are people; IPv6 has about 340 undecillion.
Every name a device here looks up goes through Pi-hole on homehub, which answers, or quietly refuses the ads.
Paul Baran in the US and Donald Davies in Britain invented packet switching independently in the 1960s; Davies named the packet.
ARPANET's first four sites, in 1969, were UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara and the University of Utah.
RFC 1, "Host Software", was written by Steve Crocker in April 1969. The series now runs past 9,000.
RFCs are "Requests for Comments" so the first ones wouldn't sound like orders. They became the internet's standards anyway.
Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published the design of TCP in 1974.
The word "internet" first appears in RFC 675 in 1974, short for "internetworking".
Telnet (1969) is older than TCP/IP, and FTP (1971) nearly as old.
SMTP, which still carries the world's email, was defined in 1982.
Usenet began in 1979 between Duke and UNC, passing news over dial-up phone links.
The first top-level domains, in 1985, were .com, .org, .net, .edu, .gov and .mil.
Canada's .ca was created in 1987.
CA*net, Canada's first national internet backbone, opened in 1990.
The World, in Boston, became the first commercial dial-up ISP in 1989.
Archie (1990) was the first internet search engine. It indexed FTP sites, not web pages.
Gopher, from the University of Minnesota in 1991, rivalled the web until licence fees scared people off.
CERN put the web into the public domain on 30 April 1993, royalty-free for anyone.
Mosaic (1993) made the web popular by showing pictures in line with the text.
The first web server, a NeXT computer at CERN, carried a label: "This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!"
The first banner ad ran on HotWired in 1994: "Have you ever clicked your mouse right here? You will."
The first thing sold on eBay, in 1995, was a broken laser pointer, for $14.83.
Hotmail launched on 4 July 1996; its capitals spelled HoTMaiL, for HTML.
The NSFNET backbone shut down in 1995, leaving the internet to commercial networks.
The Morris worm of 1988 infected thousands of computers, a sizeable share of the internet then, and led to the first CERT.
The first emoji, 176 of them, were drawn by Shigetaka Kurita for NTT DoCoMo phones in 1999.
The first tweet, in March 2006, was Jack Dorsey's "just setting up my twttr".
Wikipedia went live on 15 January 2001.
"Surfing the internet" took off from a 1992 article by librarian Jean Armour Polly.
The 1996 "ping of death": a single oversized ping could crash a computer.
GIF came from CompuServe in 1987. Its creator said it's pronounced "jif".
PNG was created in 1996 to get around a patent on GIF's compression.
MP3's inventors tuned it on Suzanne Vega's "Tom's Diner", a hard song to compress well.
The 1858 transatlantic telegraph cable lasted about three weeks; Queen Victoria's 98-word message took some 16 hours.
TAT-8, in 1988, was the first transatlantic fibre-optic cable.
Undersea cables carry repeaters every 50 to 100 km to boost the light.
Light in glass fibre travels about a third slower than in a vacuum.
Hollow-core fibre guides light through air instead of glass, and cuts delay by about a third.
Glass fibre doesn't conduct electricity: lightning can't ride a fibre line into the house.
Ethernet cable twists its pairs, each at a different rate, to cancel interference.
Gigabit Ethernet sends on all four pairs, in both directions at once.
Cat6 carries 10 Gb/s up to 55 m; Cat6a takes it the full 100 m.
Power over Ethernet can deliver up to 90 W through a network cable.
Ethernet is named after the "luminiferous ether" once thought to carry light.
"Wi-Fi" isn't short for anything: a branding firm coined it in 1999.
802.11ax became "Wi-Fi 6" in 2018, when Wi-Fi's generations got simple numbers.
IEEE 802 is the committee for networks like Ethernet (802.3) and Wi-Fi (802.11); it first met in February 1980.
Only channels 1, 6 and 11 of 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi don't overlap each other.
Microwave ovens work around 2.45 GHz, the same band as 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, which is why one can stall the other.
Wi-Fi 6E and 7 add the 6 GHz band: more room than 2.4 and 5 GHz put together.
Starlink satellites orbit at about 550 km; a geostationary one, at 35,786 km, adds about half a second to a round trip.
Copper DSL slows with distance: the closer to the exchange, the faster it runs.
A 56k modem rarely got past 53 kb/s, because of limits on line power.
Dial-up's screech was two modems testing the phone line to find the fastest speed it could bear.
PPPoE, which this house's connection uses, adds 8 bytes to each packet, so they top out at 1,492 bytes.
A gigabit line could move a 700 MB CD's worth in about six seconds.
Internet speeds are sold in bits; files are measured in bytes, eight times bigger.
Latency, not bandwidth, is what makes a page feel slow: loading one takes many round trips.
Bufferbloat: oversized buffers in routers can add whole seconds of delay when a line is busy.
IPv4 packets carry a hop limit, at most 255, so a lost packet can't circle forever.
TCP opens with a three-way handshake: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK.
Video calls use UDP, not TCP: a late frame is no use, so there's no point waiting for it again.
HTTP/3 runs over QUIC, on UDP. QUIC began at Google and became a standard in 2021.
TLS 1.3 (2018) sets up an encrypted connection in one round trip instead of two.
Let's Encrypt, since 2015, has issued billions of free certificates, which is why most of the web is HTTPS now.
NTP, from 1985, keeps clocks across the internet in step to within milliseconds.
BGP stitches the internet's tens of thousands of networks together. A BGP mistake took Facebook offline for six hours in October 2021.
In 2008 a BGP announcement from Pakistan accidentally took YouTube down worldwide.
DNSSEC signs DNS answers; the root zone was signed in 2010, its keys kept by ceremony with crypto officers on several continents.
Before DHCP (1993) there was BOOTP (1985), and before that, typing addresses in by hand.
ARP finds a device on the local network by asking everyone: "who has 10.7.1.5?"
A MAC address is 48 bits: about 281 trillion possibilities.
Phones now make up a random MAC address for each Wi-Fi network, so they can't be followed from one to the next.
NAT lets this whole house share one public IPv4 address.
The whole 127.x.x.x block, 16 million addresses, means "this computer": 127.0.0.1 is just the usual one.
A single IPv6 subnet has 18 quintillion addresses.
1.1.1.1 was so abused as a fake address that, before Cloudflare used it for DNS, researchers studied the junk sent to it.
8.8.8.8, Google's public DNS, opened in 2009.
Email finds its way by DNS MX records, around since 1986.
Ports below 1024 are "well-known": on Unix, only root may listen on them.
# Computing in general.
Ada Lovelace published what's often called the first program in 1843, for Babbage's Analytical Engine, which was never built.
Alan Turing's 1936 paper described a machine that could compute anything computable, years before any computer existed.
ENIAC, finished in 1945, weighed about 27 tonnes and ran on some 18,000 vacuum tubes.
ENIAC's first programmers were six women, among them Jean Bartik and Kathleen McNulty.
Grace Hopper's team wrote one of the first compilers, A-0, in 1952.
IBM's first hard drive, the RAMAC 350 of 1956, held about 3.75 MB on fifty 24-inch platters.
Werner Buchholz coined "byte" at IBM in 1956, spelt with a y so it couldn't be misread as "bit".
Gordon Moore predicted in 1965 that chips would double their transistors every year; in 1975 he made it every two.
The first mouse, from Douglas Engelbart's team in the 1960s, was a wooden box with two metal wheels.
Engelbart's 1968 "Mother of All Demos" showed the mouse, hypertext and video calls in a single sitting.
The Apollo Guidance Computer had about 4 KB of working memory; its programs were woven by hand into rope memory.
Intel's 4004, in 1971 the first commercial microprocessor, had 2,300 transistors.
Unix counts time in seconds from 1 January 1970; a signed 32-bit count runs out on 19 January 2038.
"Hello, world" first appeared in Brian Kernighan's writing on the B language in 1972.
The QWERTY layout comes from Christopher Latham Sholes's typewriters of the 1870s.
ASCII (1963) has 128 characters; Unicode now has more than 150,000.
VisiCalc (1979), the first spreadsheet for personal computers, sold Apple IIs to businesses on its own.
Ctrl+Alt+Del was David Bradley's IBM PC shortcut; he chose keys too far apart to press by accident.
Brain (1986), the first virus for IBM PCs, was written by two brothers in Lahore.
The 3.5-inch floppy disk, still the "save" icon, held 1.44 MB.
Linus Torvalds announced Linux in 1991 as "just a hobby, won't be big and professional".
Python is named after Monty Python, not the snake.
Brendan Eich wrote the first version of JavaScript in about ten days in 1995.
A "1 TB" drive shows as about 931 GB on Windows, which counts in powers of 1,024 rather than 1,000.
